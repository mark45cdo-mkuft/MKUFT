## Abstract and central proposal

The Voynich Manuscript is a fifteenth-century illustrated codex whose script has resisted translation despite more than a century of cryptographic, linguistic, and statistical analysis. While often described as an undeciphered text, its most striking feature is that it consistently fails when treated as language, yet remains highly structured and internally coherent.

Word-like units recur with strict positional rules, line lengths remain narrowly bounded, and symbol variation follows constrained patterns that do not align with semantics, phonetics, or syntax. These properties instead match what is seen in procedural or executable systems, where symbols encode actions, transitions, and states rather than meaning.

Structurally, the manuscript functions as a symbolic execution engine: a system designed to guide processes rather than convey narrative information. Individual glyphs behave as operators—encoding flow, repetition, holding, or reset—rather than letters. Groups of glyphs form reusable modules whose small variations alter parameters rather than meaning, analogous to adjusting steps in a recipe or phases in a process. Lines are neither arbitrary nor expressive; they are execution-bounded, consistently matching the scale of what a human could enact or track in a single continuous sequence. Pages assemble these sequences into complete procedures, while sections of the manuscript group related operational domains such as preparation, transformation, cycling, and reintegration.

## Illustration and process coupling

The illustrations reinforce this interpretation. Plants are composite and non-botanical, suggesting functional assemblies rather than species identification. Circular diagrams resemble state cycles rather than astronomical charts.

The so-called bathing figures do not depict scenes or narratives but appear embedded in flow networks, acting as markers of containment, transition, or recycling. Across the manuscript, image and script align structurally rather than descriptively: drawings anchor phases of execution, not objects to be named.

## Disciplinary position

Under this interpretation, the manuscript belongs primarily to cognitive science, specifically the study of procedural and embodied cognition—how complex “how-to” knowledge is encoded, transmitted, and executed without reliance on natural language.

It intersects directly with information theory, through its low-entropy, high-constraint symbol system; systems and control theory, through its state-transition logic and cyclic structures; history of science and medicine, as an example of pre-modern technical knowledge preservation; and non-linguistic semiotics, where symbols function as operators rather than signifiers.

It does not sit comfortably within linguistics or cryptography, and its long resistance to decoding is best understood in the archived paper as a category error rather than a failure of analysis.

## Functional interpretation

The usefulness of such a system lies in its ability to preserve and transmit complex procedural knowledge—such as medicinal preparation, physiological regulation, or cyclical treatment protocols—without requiring verbal explanation or semantic interpretation. By encoding execution rather than description, the system minimizes ambiguity, resists distortion across cultures and languages, and prevents casual misuse. Correct outcomes arise from correct execution, not from understanding symbolic “meaning,” in the same way that musical notation, choreography, or modern programming languages function.

Crucially, the archived paper argues that the manuscript operates coherently under this procedural interpretation. Attempts to treat it as language fail to produce stable semantics, grammar, or reference. Cipher models are argued to collapse under inconsistency or require implausible assumptions.

In contrast, treating the symbols as operators constrained by execution rules is proposed as a single internally consistent model that simultaneously accounts for glyph statistics, line-length constraints, page layout, illustration coupling, and cross-sectional structure. The paper’s claim is that the system is too ordered to be nonsense, too non-semantic to be prose, and too execution-bounded to be decorative; its apparent mystery persists because it has been approached as a text meant to be read rather than a system meant to be run.

## Constraint-first analytical method

The key to this interpretation was identified by reversing the initial analytical assumption. Instead of asking what the symbols “mean,” the analysis begins by asking what constraints they obey.

When line length is measured across folios, the paper reports clustering around human-executable limits rather than linguistic sentence lengths. When glyph repetition is examined, repeated groups are described as recurring with small, structured variations consistent with parameter adjustment rather than synonym replacement.

When glyph shapes are compared across contexts, the paper proposes that they retain operational roles independent of surrounding symbols, as operators do, rather than shifting meaning as words do. When illustrations are overlaid with text density and segmentation, the paper proposes alignment with execution phases rather than descriptive captions.

## Four-step independent test route

The archived manuscript gives a four-step route for independent checking:

1. Ignore phonetic or semantic assumptions entirely and treat glyphs as abstract operators.
2. Measure and compare line lengths and repetition patterns across unrelated sections to test for execution bounds rather than narrative variation.
3. Examine whether repeated glyph clusters behave as reusable modules with constrained deformation rather than as words with flexible meaning.
4. Test whether page layouts and illustrations align with process stages rather than with depicted objects.

The paper’s stated conclusion is that, under these constraints, the procedural interpretation remains globally consistent while alternative readings fragment across sections.
