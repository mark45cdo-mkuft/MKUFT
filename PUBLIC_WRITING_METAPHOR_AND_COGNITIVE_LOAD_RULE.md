# Public Writing — Metaphor and Cognitive-Load Rule

**Status:** standing reader-interface discipline for live public MKUFT writing. This is a writing rule, not scientific evidence, mechanism, ontology, or a licence to alter technical claims.

## Purpose

MKUFT contains objects that are simple in relational shape but expensive to reconstruct from compressed technical prose. A good metaphor can lower that reconstruction cost without lowering the scientific resolution.

The governing rule is:

> **Use metaphor when one ordinary picture can carry a load-bearing relation across a genuine conceptual choke point more cheaply than another paragraph of abstraction. Then stop the picture at its earned boundary and return to the native scientific object.**

The target is lower **unnecessary cognitive load**, not lower intellectual demand.

## Metaphor is a carrier, not decoration

A metaphor earns inclusion only when it does at least one of the following:

- exposes a relation that technical vocabulary otherwise makes unnecessarily hard to see;
- preserves a distinction across a cross-domain handoff;
- lets a capable reader predict the next formal move before the terminology arrives;
- reduces the number of new objects the reader must hold simultaneously;
- gives a stable handle to a relation that will recur later in the reader's wake.

Vividness alone is not sufficient.

Do not add a metaphor merely because the prose feels dry. Do not replace a precise explanation with a story. Do not stack several metaphors over the same relation. Do not keep changing pictures after the reader has earned a stable handle.

## Bounded-crossing rule

For a candidate metaphor `m` carrying relation `r`:

```text
identify the exact relation r the reader must cross
→ ask whether current prose imposes avoidable reconstruction load
→ if no, keep the native prose
→ if yes, choose the smallest familiar picture that preserves r
→ state or make obvious the exact point where the picture stops
→ return to the native term / equation / experiment
→ preserve the reader's earned handle thereafter
```

A metaphor fails if the scientific claim becomes true only inside the picture.

A metaphor succeeds when it can be removed after the crossing and the reader can still reconstruct the same technical relation, evidence state, failure condition and mechanism boundary.

## The consequence-cone example

The native consequence-cone object is a useful calibration for this rule.

The technical claim is not that experiments possess literal cones. It is that a load-bearing relation should be stressed inside the smallest native environment that still preserves the downstream relations needed to tell what the break means. A test fixture that is too thin can produce a dramatic but uninformative break; an uncontrolled environment that is too rich can hide ownership through compensation and collateral coupling.

A clean picture is to imagine testing one cable in a suspension bridge. Cutting a loose cable on a workshop floor proves that the cable can be cut. Cutting or unloading the corresponding member in a controlled structural test, while preserving the relevant load paths and measuring where force redistributes, can tell you whether that member was load-bearing, what compensated, and whether the larger structure lost closure. The picture stops at **native load path, compensation and observable consequence**. It does not imply that biological, AI or physical systems are bridges, or that the cone is a literal spatial structure.

After that crossing, return immediately to the native object:

```text
load-bearing relation r
→ smallest target-sufficient native consequence surface
→ predicted deformation signature
→ matched compensation / boundary / generic-damage controls
→ restore / retest
→ smallest surviving owner
```

## Avoid the Aesop failure

Public science should not become a sequence of parables.

Warning signs:

- every subsection opens with a story;
- the reader remembers the picture but not the scientific relation;
- characters or moral lessons are doing evidential work;
- the metaphor needs repeated aftercare or exceptions;
- several pictures compete for the same technical object;
- the prose keeps explaining a relation the reader has already earned;
- a metaphor is used where one direct sentence is clearer.

The preferred density is **one strong crossing picture at a real choke point, zero where the native relation is already obvious**.

## Reader wake

Metaphor must respect accumulated reader wake.

Once a picture has earned a technical relation, the later text should normally reuse the native handle rather than replaying the story. Reintroduce the picture only if the audience, scale or operation has changed enough that the old carrier no longer closes the crossing.

```text
reader has earned relation r through picture m
→ retain r in wake
→ retire m from foreground
→ add next delta
```

This prevents a helpful metaphor from becoming permanent cognitive furniture.

## Scientific hygiene

Metaphor may change:

- accessibility;
- reconstruction cost;
- salience;
- memory of the dependency structure.

Metaphor may not change:

- evidence level;
- claim scope;
- mechanism;
- ontology;
- units;
- causal attribution;
- falsifier;
- permission;
- result.

Where the metaphor and native science disagree, the native science wins and the metaphor is repaired or removed.

## Standing audit for public-body edits

Whenever live public MKUFT prose is created or materially revised, inspect each genuine conceptual choke point:

1. What exact relation must the reader carry next?
2. Has the reader already earned it?
3. Is the present difficulty scientific, or merely reconstruction load caused by compressed prose?
4. Would one ordinary picture preserve the relation more cheaply?
5. Can the picture's stopping point be stated cleanly?
6. Does the prose return to the native owner immediately afterward?
7. Does the picture preserve the same claim boundary and falsifier?
8. Is the metaphor still needed after the reader has crossed?

Add, retain or remove metaphor according to those answers. There is no quota.

## Recursive learning

When a metaphor repeatedly succeeds or fails across reader tests, extract the transferable relation rather than blindly propagating the wording.

```text
reader friction
→ locate conceptual choke point
→ test direct prose versus bounded picture
→ preserve whichever lowers reconstruction load without semantic loss
→ carry reusable lesson into standing writing discipline
→ re-audit affected reader route
```

Do not standardise a metaphor merely because it once worked. Standardise the **reason it worked**.

## Compression

> **A good scientific metaphor is temporary scaffolding at a conceptual crossing. It should let the reader carry more of the real structure with less unnecessary effort, then get out of the way. One clean picture can be rigorous translation; a parade of pictures is just another cognitive load.**
